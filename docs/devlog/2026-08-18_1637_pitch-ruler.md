# Dev log — vertical pitch ruler

A second tuner display, sitting below the needle on the Tuner tab.

## What it is
A vertical scale of every semitone from C2 to C6 in concert pitch, with a
marker showing where the current pitch actually sits.

The needle answers "how far off this note am I". The ruler answers "where am I",
which is the question the needle can't: an octave error looks identical on a
needle and obvious on a ruler.

## Decisions
- **C2–C6, not C1–C8.** Asked for "C1 to C8 or something", but the detector only
  hears roughly B1 to D6, and a sax or a voice lives in a much narrower band
  still. Five octaves gives each semitone about 12px of real estate instead of
  7, and nothing is drawn that can never be reached.
- **Below the needle, same tab**, so fine tuning and absolute position are
  visible together without switching.
- **The marker is not snapped to the nearest note.** It sits at the true pitch,
  so you can see yourself sitting between two notes and drifting.
- **Ticks are graded**: every semitone faint, naturals brighter and labelled,
  the C of each octave brightest and bold. Labelling all 49 would be a mess;
  labelling only the Cs would make notes hard to locate.
- **Concert pitch on the scale**, consistent with the needle display now leading
  with concert. The marker pill carries both, e.g. `G4 · A5 -1`.

## Notes
- Out of range clamps to the end of the scale and says so, rather than letting
  the marker vanish silently off the edge.
- The scale is inset 11px top and bottom inside its box. Without that the C6 and
  C2 labels, which centre on their line, were clipped by the border — caught by
  looking at it, not by any test.
- Nothing needed rebuilding when the instrument changes, since the scale is in
  concert pitch and only the pill text is transposed.

Also checked: 49 ticks, 29 labelled naturals, five bold octave lines C2–C6;
A3 twenty cents sharp landed at 55.83% against the A3 line at 56.25%, exactly
the 0.42% a fifth of a semitone should be; B1 clamped to the bottom reading
"below the ruler" and D6 to the top reading "above the ruler"; the marker hides
on silence and on stopping the tuner; and both end labels measured inside the
container after the inset fix.

## Octave numbering, checked against a phone tuner

First cross-check against a phone app looked alarming: it called a note F3 where
we called it F5. It turned out to be notation, not detection — both read the
same frequency, 311 Hz.

That note is E♭4 concert, fingered F on tenor, so our two columns read `E♭4`
and `F5`.

My first guess — that the app numbered octaves from the horn's own low B♭ — was
wrong. It is two separate one-octave effects stacking, and the player worked it
out: their app was set to a generic **B♭** transposition.

There are two B♭ transpositions, an octave apart. Tenor is written a major ninth
above what sounds, +14 semitones; clarinet, trumpet and soprano sax are written
a whole tone above, +2. A generic "B♭" setting means the +2 kind, so concert E♭4
reads F4 rather than F5. Then Yamaha-style numbering, middle C as C3, drops that
to F3.

Both of our choices stay: tenor really is a +14 instrument, and scientific pitch
notation is what tuners use. See `CHALLENGES.md`.

Worth remembering as a diagnostic: **compare hertz before believing a note-name
disagreement.** Matching frequencies mean the argument is only about labels.

Also ruled out along the way — the detector holds the correct octave on a low
tone even with the fundamental attenuated to nothing, which is what YIN is for:

```
Fed F3 = 174.61 Hz
  fundamental 100% -> 174.5 Hz  F3
  fundamental   5% -> 174.5 Hz  F3
  fundamental   0% -> 174.5 Hz  F3
```
